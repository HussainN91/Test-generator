### Grammar: Present Simple & Present Continuous
*   **Present Simple**: Used for things that are always true or happen regularly. Often used with words like *often, usually, every day, twice a week*.
    *   *Form*: Subject + Verb (adds *-s*, *-es*, or *-ies* for third-person singular).
        *   *Most verbs*: add *-s* (e.g., *learns, works*)
        *   *Verbs ending in -o, -s, -sh, -ch, -x, -zz*: add *-es* (e.g., *goes, watches*)
        *   *Verbs ending in consonant + -y*: remove *-y* and add *-ies* (e.g., *studies*)
        *   *Irregular*: *have* → *has*
*   **Present Continuous**: Used for things happening now or at the present moment. Often used with words like *now, today, this week, at the moment*.
    *   *Form*: Subject + am/is/are + Verb-ing.
        *   *Most verbs*: add *-ing* (e.g., *playing*)
        *   *Verbs ending in -e*: remove *-e* and add *-ing* (e.g., *living, making*)
        *   *Verbs ending in -ie*: change *-ie* to *-y* and add *-ing* (e.g., *lying*)
        *   *One-syllable verbs ending in vowel + consonant*: double the consonant and add *-ing* (e.g., *sitting, swimming*)
        *   *Two-syllable verbs ending in stressed vowel + consonant*: double the consonant (e.g., *beginning*, but *opening*)
        *   *British English doubling*: *travel* → *travelling*

### Grammar: State Verbs (Non-Continuous Verbs)
*   These verbs are not normally used in the continuous form:
    *   **Verbs of thinking**: *believe, understand, know, think, mean, hope* (e.g., *Scientists believe...*)
    *   **Verbs of liking/disliking**: *like, hate, love, want, need, prefer* (e.g., *I like those monkeys...*)
    *   **Verbs of owning**: *own, belong, have* (e.g., *That coat belongs to me...*)
    *   **Verbs of sensation**: *see, feel, hear, smell, taste* (e.g., *The water feels cold...*)
*   **Special Exceptions**:
    *   We can use continuous form to say how someone looks or feels *now* (e.g., *I'm feeling better* or *I feel better*).
    *   We often use *can* with sensation verbs (e.g., *I can hear the sea*).
    *   When *think* means "have an opinion," do not use the continuous (e.g., *I think it's good*). When *think* means "consider," the continuous is allowed (e.g., *I'm thinking of working in a zoo*).

### Grammar: Past Simple
*   Used to talk about completed events in the past.
    *   *Positive regular*: Subject + Verb-ed (e.g., *climbed*)
    *   *Positive irregular*: Subject + Past Simple Form (e.g., *knew*)
    *   *Negative*: Subject + didn't + Verb (e.g., *didn't want*)
    *   *Questions*: Did + Subject + Verb? (e.g., *Did you travel?*)
    *   *Spelling (regular)*:
        *   *Most verbs*: add *-ed* (e.g., *played, climbed*)
        *   *Verbs ending in -e*: add *-d* (e.g., *decided, arrived*)
        *   *Verbs ending in consonant + -y*: change *-y* to *-i* and add *-ed* (e.g., *carried*)
        *   *One-syllable verbs ending in vowel + consonant*: double the consonant and add *-ed* (e.g., *stopped*)
        *   *Two-syllable verbs ending in stressed vowel + consonant*: double the consonant (e.g., *preferred*)
        *   *British English doubling*: *travel* → *travelled*

### Grammar: Past Continuous & Past Simple
*   **Past Continuous**: Used to describe activities happening at a particular moment in the past. Multiple actions can happen at the same time.
    *   *Form*: Subject + was/were + Verb-ing (e.g., *eating, standing*).
*   **Combining Past Continuous & Past Simple**:
    *   Use **Past Continuous** after *while* to refer to a longer action that occurred at the same time as another action.
    *   Use **Past Simple** when actions occur sequentially (one after another).
    *   Use **Past Simple** after *when* for a shorter action that interrupts a longer, ongoing past action (e.g., *I was putting on my coat when it started to rain*).

### Grammar: Comparative & Superlative Adverbs
*   Adjectives describe nouns; adverbs describe verbs (most end in *-ly* like *slowly*, but some do not like *fast, hard, late, well*).
*   **Comparative Adverbs**: Used to compare two things.
    *   Use *more + adverb* for adverbs ending in *-ly* (e.g., *more carefully*). Opposite: *less*.
    *   Add *-er* to short adverbs (e.g., *faster, harder, later*).
    *   Irregular: *well* → *better*, *badly* → *worse*.
    *   *Often* comparison: *more often*.
    *   Use *(not) as + adverb + as* for equal or unequal comparisons.
*   **Superlative Adverbs**: Used to compare one thing with two or more others.
    *   Use *the most + adverb* for adverbs ending in *-ly* (e.g., *the most slowly*). Opposite: *the least*.
    *   Add *-est* to short adverbs (e.g., *the fastest, the hardest, the latest*).
    *   Irregular: *well* → *the best*, *badly* → *the worst*.
    *   *Often* comparison: *the most often*.

### Grammar: Possession (Apostrophes, Determiners & Pronouns)
*   **Apostrophe + s ('s)**:
    *   Used for people and animals.
    *   *Singular nouns*: add *'s* (e.g., *my mum's necklace*).
    *   *Plural nouns ending in -s*: add apostrophe only (e.g., *my friends' shoes*).
    *   *Irregular plural nouns not ending in -s*: add *'s* (e.g., *the children's bedroom*).
*   **Possessive Determiners vs. Possessive Pronouns**:
    *   *Determiners* (followed by noun): *my, your, his, her, our, their*.
    *   *Pronouns* (replace determiner + noun): *mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirs* (e.g., *Is this your jacket? No, it's hers*).
    *   **Double Possessive**: Use *'a friend of mine/yours/his/hers/ours/theirs'* (e.g., *a classmate of his*, not *a classmate of him*).

*   **Must / Mustn't**: Used for rules and obligations.
    *   *Must*: Obligation to do something (e.g., *You must switch off your mobile phone*).

### Grammar: Verb Patterns – Gerunds & Infinitives
*   When using two verbs together, the second is usually a gerund (*-ing*) or an infinitive (*to + verb*).
    *   **Verb + Gerund**: *finish, don't mind, enjoy, miss*
    *   **Verb + Infinitive**: *decide, choose, learn, help, hope, plan, want, need*
    *   **Verb + Gerund OR Infinitive**: *start, begin, prefer, like, love* (no major change in meaning)
*   **Gerund after Prepositions**: Use a gerund after prepositions like *at, in, for, of, about* (e.g., *Thank you for inviting me*, *I'm thinking of buying...*).
*   **Subject Gerund**: A gerund can be the subject of a verb (e.g., *Joining the film club was...*).

### Grammar: Present Perfect with Ever & Never
*   Used to talk about life experiences before the present moment.
    *   *Form*: Subject + have/has (not) + past participle (regular verbs end in *-ed*, irregulars vary).
    *   **ever**: Used in questions to mean 'at any time in your life' (e.g., *Have you ever climbed a tree?*).
    *   **never**: Used in positive sentences to mean 'not at any time in your life' (e.g., *My grandparents have never flown...*).
    *   *Contrast*: Do not use past time expressions (like *yesterday, last year*) with the Present Perfect. Use them with the Past Simple.

### Grammar: Present Perfect with Just, Yet, & Already
*   **just**: Indicates that something happened a very short time ago. Placed between *have/has* and the past participle (e.g., *I've just eaten*).
*   **already**: States that something happened before now, often sooner than expected. Placed between *have/has* and the past participle (e.g., *I've already done it*).
*   **yet**: Used to ask or talk about time until now (expecting it to happen in the future). Placed at the end of questions or negative sentences (e.g., *Have you been there yet? / I haven't been yet*).

### Grammar: Present Perfect vs. Past Simple
*   **Present Perfect**:
    *   Finished action where the *result* is important now (no interest in when it happened).
    *   Life experiences up to now (with *ever/never*).
    *   Recent actions (with *just/already/yet*).
    *   Actions continuing from past to present (with *for/since*).
*   **Past Simple**:
    *   Actions completely finished in the past.
    *   When the specific *time* of the action is important or mentioned (e.g., *last week*, *yesterday*).
    *   Finished past periods (e.g., *When did you learn...?*).

### Grammar: First Conditional
*   Used to describe real or highly probable future situations and their results.
    *   *Structure*: **If + Present Simple, will/won't + infinitive** (e.g., *If you study, you will pass*).
    *   *Reversed Structure*: **will/won't + infinitive if + Present Simple** (e.g., *You will pass if you study*).
    *   *Punctuation*: Use a comma if the "If" clause comes first. No comma is used if the "If" clause is in the middle.
    *   *Negatives*: Can be used in either or both clauses (e.g., *If you don't finish, you won't know*).
